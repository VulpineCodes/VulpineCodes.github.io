About

Systems on one side, people on the other. I work in the middle.

I grew up in Lagos, Nigeria — a city that runs on ingenuity. My father ran a small pharmacy there, and watching him keep a business moving with paper ledgers, phone calls, and sheer resourcefulness gave me an early, concrete sense of what operations actually are: the systems underneath the thing everyone sees. Years later, that pharmacy became the inspiration for PharmaFlow, my data-platform project.

At Vanderbilt I study computer science and cognitive psychology together, and the pairing is the point. Computer science taught me how systems behave; cognitive studies taught me to ask how people perceive, decide, and err. I build software at that intersection — fraud tooling that respects an investigator's attention, travel planning that shows its reasoning, VR that understands how a body feels inside a virtual room.

The work has ranged widely: a fraud investigation tool at Microsoft that turned hour-long investigations into five-second ones; VR paradigms for neuroscience experiments in a Meta-funded lab; an AI travel assistant that started as an externship and grew into an exercise in trustworthy AI. Different domains, same habit — understand the system, respect the human, verify the result.

Outside the terminal I write (the H.H. Morgan Memorial Award for Writers sits oddly but happily next to the research fellowship), draw, and follow art, comics, and design. That isn't decoration on an engineering profile — visual taste and storytelling are part of how I design interfaces and experiences, from an editorial donation platform to a karaoke stage in VR.

I'm targeting new-grad software engineering roles for 2027, in full-stack and backend engineering, data and infrastructure, and AI applications — with immersive technology as the thread that makes the rest more human.
